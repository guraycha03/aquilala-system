1.1 Project Context
Agriculture is the backbone of economies worldwide, providing food, raw materials, and
employment for billions of people [1]. Globally, digital technologies such as artificial intelligence,
the Internet of Things, and data analytics are increasingly being adopted to improve farming
efficiency, reduce costs, and enhance crop yields [2]. However, in many developing countries,
smallholder farmers still rely on traditional practices, leading to suboptimal outcomes.
In Southeast Asia, agriculture remains a critical sector for economic development and rural
livelihoods. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) has recognized the importance
of digital transformation in agriculture, promoting initiatives that leverage technology to enhance
food security and farm productivity [3]. Countries such as Vietnam, Thailand, and Indonesia
have begun implementing digital agricultural platforms to connect farmers with markets, provide extension services, and facilitate access to farm inputs [4]. Nevertheless, adoption rates
remain uneven, and many smallholder farmers continue to face barriers related to infrastructure,
affordability, and digital literacy [5].

In the Philippines, agriculture is a vital sector, contributing significantly to employment
and rural development [6]. The national government has promoted various digital agriculture
initiatives, such as the SAAD (Special Area for Agricultural Development) program and Project
SARAi (Smarter Approaches to Reinvigorate Agriculture as an Industry), to modernize farming
practices [7][8]. Despite these efforts, many Filipino farmers continue to depend on manual
processes, word-of-mouth information, and rough estimates for planning and budgeting [9].
Focusing on the province of Sorsogon in the Bicol Region, agriculture is the primary source
of livelihood for the majority of rural households. Within Sorsogon, the municipality of Bulan
is identified as an agricultural area where farming serves as one of the main sources of income
for local residents. Farmers in Bulan typically follow two major cropping seasons: the Wet Season (June to October) and the Dry Season (November to May). During the wet season, farmers
cultivate rice varieties that are tolerant to flooding and heavy rainfall. During the dry season,
farmers may plant rice, corn, or high-value crops depending on water availability from irrigation
systems. The Aquilala Irrigators Association plays a critical role in managing water distribution
for these seasonal plantings. Local farmers still depend on traditional methods, personal experience, and word-of-mouth information when making farming decisions. Because of this, planning
is often not well-structured, and farmers may end up choosing the wrong materials or spending
more than expected.
The Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated is a farmer-owned organization operating
within Bulan, Sorsogon. According to an informal assessment conducted by the proponents in
coordination with association officers, the association currently manages its farm supply inventory using manual logs and basic spreadsheets. Farmers who are members of the association and
need supplies such as seeds, fertilizers, or pesticides must physically visit the association store or
contact store personnel to inquire about product availability and pricing. This manual approach
leads to delays, inaccurate stock information, and inefficiencies in serving farmers.
In addition, many farmers struggle with planning their farming activities across the wet and
dry seasons. Without structured guidance, farmers often rely on personal experience or word-ofmouth when deciding which supplies to buy and in what quantities for each season.To address
this, the proposed system integrates an LLM-Driven Farm Input Planning Module that leverages
an underlying rule-based reasoning engine to provide conversational, season-specific guidance for
farm input selection based on crop type, land area, and season. Cost estimation is done roughly,
leading to budget miscalculations or incomplete preparation. Farmers also lack a systematic way
to document actual harvest yields and compare them with their planned outputs across different
seasons, making it difficult for the association to assess the effectiveness of recommended inputs
and provide better guidance for future planting cycles. The respondents expressed a strong
need for a single, accessible platform that could provide user account management for three

types of users: Admin (who oversees the entire system), Store Owner (who manages products
and inventory), and Farmers (who purchase supplies and report harvests), along with farming
cost quotation, product catalog management, farm product and store recommendations, and

At present, available systems usually focus on only one aspect, such as an online marketplace or general farming information. Few platforms integrate role-specific user management for
Admin, Store Owner, and Farmers, along with product catalog browsing, cost quotation generation, decision support for farm input planning, and comprehensive harvest reporting with

1.2 Purpose and Description
The purpose of the study is to address the common challenges experienced by the Aquilala
Irrigators Association Incorporated and its farmers in Bulan, Sorsogon, particularly in managing user accounts for Admin, Store Owner, and Farmers, generating farming cost quotations,
managing store products, receiving farm product and store recommendations, and generating
comprehensive harvest reports with seasonal data visualization. Many farmers still rely on manual processes and rough estimates, which often leads to disorganized planning and inefficient
use of resources, as supported by studies indicating challenges in the adoption and use of digital decision support tools in farming-related decision-making processes [12], [13].To address this,
the proposed web-based platform provides a unified workflow for farm supply management and
automated farm input planning by integrating an automated rule-based calculation engine that
provides structured, formula-driven guidance for farm input selection based on crop type, land
area, and season.
The proposed system follows a sequential business process that demonstrates understanding of the association’s operations across the wet and dry seasons. The system has three types
of users with different access levels. The Admin has full access to all system functions including user management, system configuration, and overall oversight. The Store Owner manages
product listings, updates inventory levels, sets product prices, and receives low-stock alerts. The
Farmers (association members) can browse the product catalog, receive farm product and store
recommendations based on their crop type, land area, and selected season (Wet Season or Dry
Season), request cost quotations, submit harvest reports, and view comparative analytics.
In Step 1 (Administrative Setup), the Admin registers Store Owners and Farmer accounts,
while the Store Owner inputs base product prices, inventory levels, and crop-specific input
formulas which are the standard application rates per hectare for each crop type. The system
also allows configuration of season-specific parameters, recognizing that input requirements may
differ between wet and dry seasons. In Step 2 (Farmer Input), a registered Farmer selects their
crop type such as Rice, selects the farming season (Wet Season or Dry Season), and inputs their
land area such as 1 hectare. In Step 3 (Automated Supply Formulation), the system processes this
data using a Rule-Based Algorithm. For example, if the crop is Rice, the season is Dry Season,
and the land area is 1 hectare, then the system recommends 2 bags of Urea Fertilizer and 20 kilos
of seeds. If the season is Wet Season, the system may recommend a different seed variety that is
flood-tolerant.The generated recommendations are then enhanced through the Large Language
Model (LLM), which converts the structured outputs into clearer, user-friendly explanations
and recommendations. This ensures that the outputs are easier for Farmers, Store Owners,
and Admin to understand while still being based on standard agricultural application rates. In
4
Step 4 (Quotation Generation), the system generates an itemized cost quotation based on the
recommended inputs multiplied by the current association prices, with each quotation including a
”Valid Until” date typically set to 3 to 7 days to protect against price fluctuations. The quotation
also records the selected season (Wet or Dry) for future reference. If a quotation expires, the
Farmer can simply generate a new quotation which will reflect the current prices and stock
availability. In Step 5 (Feedback Loop), after harvest, the Farmer submits actual yield data and
actual expenses, specifying which season the harvest corresponds to (Wet Season or Dry Season).
The system then compares predicted versus actual output, refining future recommendations.
The system then generates comprehensive reports including harvest yield and planned output
comparison using line charts with seasonal markers, cost quotation and actual farm expense
analysis using KPI cards and charts, crop performance per season (comparing Wet Season vs.
Dry Season performance) using line charts, and Farm Output Performance Report using KPI
cards. These specific UI elements (line charts and KPI cards) will be strictly delivered as stated
in Objective 2.5.
To improve how results are presented to users, the proposed system integrates the Groq
Cloud API (Llama 3.3 Model) which serves as a support component that processes structured
system outputs and converts them into clearer explanations, recommendations, and user-friendly
responses. This Large Language Model (LLM) integration ensures that system-calculated supply
plans, quotations, and reports are presented in natural, conversational language. This support
component is not a main feature but rather an enhancement that ensures system-generated
outputs are presented in natural, easy-to-understand language to Farmers, Store Owners, and
Admin. The Groq Cloud API is an external service that handles natural language generation
and output formatting separately from the core system business logic. In the event of API downtime, the system includes a fallback mechanism that displays basic formatted text without LLM
enhancement, ensuring core functionality remains accessible.Thus, the integration of a RuleBased Algorithm for accurate calculations and a Large Language Model (LLM) for user-friendly
explanations and output enhancement provides a robust, accessible, and reliable platform for
the Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated.

1.4 Scope and Limitation
This study focuses on the development of a Web-Based Farm Supply Management System with Large Language Model designed for the Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated in
Bulan, Sorsogon. This study also incorporates a Large Language Model (LLM) via Groq Cloud
API to enhance output presentation and user comprehension, alongside rule-based algorithms
for farm input recommendations. The system is intended for use by three types of users: Admin
(system administrator who oversees the entire system), Store Owner (manages products and
inventory), and Farmers (registered association members involved in farm supply procurement,
farming activities, and harvest documentation). The system includes features such as identification of needed data, user account management with three roles, farming cost quotation (with
”Valid Until” dates and season selection for Wet or Dry season), store and product management, farm product and store recommendations (using rule-based algorithms that account for
seasonal variations), and comprehensive report generation of actual harvest including harvest
yield vs. planned output comparison using line charts with seasonal markers, cost quotation
vs. actual expense analysis using KPI cards and charts, crop performance per season using line
charts comparing Wet Season vs. Dry Season, and Farm Output Performance Report using KPI
cards.The system provides structured, rule-based guidance for farm input selection based on
crop type, land area, and season, transforming raw data into actionable recommendations. The
system also integrates the Groq Cloud API (Llama 3.3 Model) as a support component that
processes structured system outputs and converts them into clearer explanations, recommendations, and user-friendly responses. This Large Language Model (LLM) component ensures that
system outputs, quotations, and reports are presented in natural, conversational language. This
external API handles natural language generation and output formatting separately from the
core system business logic. The LLM serves as an enhancement layer only, not a core dependency.
However, the study has several limitations framed as design focus rather than weaknesses. The data identified and listed are focused specifically on the operational context of
Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated in Bulan, Sorsogon, and while this ensures relevance
and accuracy for the target users, the specific data parameters such as crop types, application
rates, seasonal variations, and pricing structures may require reconfiguration for application to
other associations or agricultural settings. Similarly, the automated logical rule-sets (e.g., if crop
is Rice and season is Dry Season, then recommend 2 bags of Urea) are specific to the association’s recommended application rates and may need adjustment for other contexts. The system
is designed exclusively for registered Farmers of Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated
and does not extend to non-members or other associations, with user registration for Farmers

requiring Admin verification and authentication limited to standard password-based login without biometric or two-factor mechanisms. Store Owners are registered by the Admin, and the
Admin account is predefined for system oversight.
Financial computations are limited to gross cost estimations based on association unit

and other ancillary costs are outside the current system architecture. Quotations generated by

2.1 Identification of Data Needed for Agricultural Systems.
The study titled ”AgriNet Smart Centralized Platform for Fertilizer, Labour and Supply Management” published in the International Journal of Advanced Multidisciplinary Research
and Educational Development (IJAMRED) in 2025 conducted a systematic literature review
of existing digital agricultural platforms [2]. The authors analyzed databases such as IEEE
Xplore, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, selecting studies published between 2015 and 2025
that emphasize IoT-based monitoring, cloud computing, and data-driven decision-making in
agriculture. Their survey revealed that while numerous systems exist for individual tasks like
soil monitoring or logistics, few provide a unified solution for end-to-end management, creating
a significant integration gap where farmers must use multiple disconnected tools for different
tasks. None of the reviewed systems accounted for seasonal variations (wet vs. dry seasons) in
their recommendation logic or reporting. The proposed system for Aquilala Irrigators Association Incorporated directly addresses this gap by integrating user account management, farming
cost quotation with season selection, store and product management, farm product and store
recommendations with season-specific rules, and comprehensive harvest reporting with seasonal
data visualization into a single, unified web-based platform.
”Customization of Agricultural Technology Packages: A CPQ-Based Approach to
Smart Farming” by Kishore Kumar Epuri, published in the Journal of Information Systems Engineering and Management in 2025, explored the convergence of Configure-Price-Quote (CPQ)
frameworks with intelligent agricultural systems [3]. The study demonstrated that CPQ architectures enable precise assembly of smart farming components through granular configuration
capabilities, yet these frameworks are typically designed for commercial product configuration
and lack integration with agricultural-specific data such as crop types, land area, seasonal planting cycles, and wet/dry season variations. The proposed system bridges this gap by adapting
the CPQ principle to agriculture, linking crop type, land area, and season (Wet or Dry) inputs
directly to cost quotation generation, ensuring that quotations are based on actual farming
requirements that vary by season rather than generic product selections, directly mapping to
Objective 2.2.

Smart Digital Agribusiness Marketplace” by Prabowo, Subiyanto, and Salim, published in
Kinetik: Game Technology, Information System, Computer Network, Computing, Electronics,

gains of approximately 4.9 percent in Precision, 5.9 percent in Recall, and 5.6 percent in F1-
Score. However, while GMNCF achieves high accuracy, it requires machine learning expertise
and substantial computational resources, making it impractical for small association implementations where technical capacity is limited. Moreover, it does not incorporate seasonal logic.

details, inventory records, crop types, land area, pricing, quotation history, harvest records,
and report templates. Requirements gathering is conducted through interviews with cooperative
officers, store staff, and farmers. The team then plans five sprints each lasting two weeks.
Design Phase.The design phase is the stage where system requirements are translated
into a blueprint for construction. It involves creating architectural designs, database schemas,
user interface layouts, and component specifications.Designing a friendly user interface using
Figma with low and high-fidelity wireframes of the web-based application. The user interface
prioritizes user experience and usability for Admin, Store Owner, and Farmers with clear text,
clean visual hierarchy, proper color palette and contrasts, and following the Swiss design principles of less is more and simplicity. The system has a responsive design for usage even on remote
areas, allowing farmers to use the system even with slow internet.
Development Phase. The development phase is the stage where the actual coding
and implementation of the system occur. The development is intended to have quick and short
sprint phases focusing on specific features of the system and iterating based on the features set
by the user, allowing for flexible development of the application. The development is divided into
five sprints: Sprint 1 builds User Account Management for Admin, Store Owner, and Farmers;
Sprint 2 builds Store and Product Management including product catalog, inventory tracking,
and low-stock alerts; Sprint 3 builds Farm Product and Store Recommendation using a RuleBased Algorithm (e.g., if crop is Rice and land area is 1 hectare, then recommend 2 bags of Urea)
and Farming Cost Quotation with ”Valid Until” dates; Sprint 4 builds Report Generation of
33
Actual Harvest with data visualization using line charts and KPI cards, including harvest yield
vs. planned output comparison, cost quotation vs. actual expense analysis, crop performance per
season, and Farm Output Performance Report; Sprint 5 integrates all modules, tests the system,
and prepares for deployment.
Testing Phase. The testing phase is the stage where the developed system is evaluated
to verify that it meets specified requirements and to identify defects. Analyzing that the application is working as it is intended to be, testing all of the functionalities and features allowing to
identify problems, bugs to be fixed at the next iteration week. The usability of the application
will both be tested by the developers and by Admin, Store Owner, and Farmers at the end of
each two-week iteration to check if the application is working as intended.
Deployment Phase.The deployment phase is the stage where the completed system
is released to the production environment for use by end-users. It involves installing the system,
configuring the production environment, migrating data, training users, and providing ongoing
support. After the continual development, test, and refinement, the system is deployed to the
Aquilala Farmers Cooperative for use by Admin, Store Owner, and a small number of farmers for
further feedback, providing tutorials on the system’s functionality and logic to be implemented.
Using the feedback, the developers will rollout bug fixes and patches to the application for
smooth deployment.
4.4 Development Approach
The Top-Down approach is intended to be implemented in the development of the
application/system, the top-down approach begin at the highest level of the system and progressively breaks down the system into smaller parts allowing easier planning, documentation,
smooth, and early development of the application.

4.10 Testing
The system underwent a structured software testing process to ensure that all functionalities operate correctly, meet the requirements of the Aquilala Irrigators Association
Incorporated, and and provide accurate recommendations and quotations prior to deployment.
The testing process was divided into three phases: unit testing, integration testing, and user
acceptance testing (UAT).
Unit Testing focused on verifying the smallest components of the system to ensure
correct functionality at the code level. Core modules such as user authentication, product catalog
management, farm input validation, and cost computation logic were individually tested. This
ensured that the Laravel 12 backend correctly processes inputs, such as computing costs for
specific farm inputs, without dependency on other system modules.
Integration Testing examined how different modules work together. This phase ensured
that the farm input module properly communicates with the quotation engine, and that the
recommendation module correctly retrieves relevant product data from the MySQL database.
It also verified the consistency of data flow across the system’s layered architecture, from user
input to quotation generation.
User Acceptance Testing (UAT) was the final phase. In this stage, the system was
evaluated by actual users, including farmers and store owners, to assess its performance in realworld scenarios. Farmers tested the system by generating cost quotations based on crop type and
42
land area, while store owners evaluated product management and demand monitoring features.
This phase focused on usability, accuracy, and overall system effectiveness. Feedback from users
was used to make final refinements before system deployment.
