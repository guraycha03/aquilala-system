To build an accurate system plan, it helps to understand that an Irrigators Association (IA) operates like a cooperative network rather than a single corporate farm. It is a digital and operational matrix of independent pieces connected by a shared resource: water.A structural breakdown shows how the Aquilala Irrigators Association operates across these specific areas:1. Land Ownership: One Big Plot or Separated?Divided, Fragmented Lands: It is not one massive, contiguous plantation. It consists of hundreds of separated, individually managed rice paddies spread across local barangays or farming zones.Owned vs. Rented: The land tenure is mixed. Some members are owner-cultivators (they own the land outright), while many are tenants or leaseholders who rent the land and pay a percentage of their harvest to a landlord.The System Insight: In your system database, a "Member" profile shouldn't just map to a single user. It needs to support multiple disconnected land parcels (often called parcelles or farm lots), each with its own size, location, and tenure status (Owned vs. Rented).2. How Planting and Water Mapping WorksSector Zoning (Turn-Out Groups): The association divides the entire service area into smaller zones called Turn-Out Groups (TOGs). Each group is managed by a Terminal Group Leader (one of the officers).Synchronized Planting Calendar: Because water is scarce, farmers cannot just plant whenever they want. The association creates a strict cropping calendar based on water releases from the main NIA dam.The Flow: Sector A gets water first → Sector A plows and plants → Water shifts to Sector B → Sector B plants.The System Insight: Your system plan will need a Water Scheduling or Cropping Calendar module. It should track which geographic sectors are currently in the Land Preparation, Planting, Growing, or Harvesting phases to help predict water demand and allocation.3. Meetings and GovernanceThe association relies heavily on face-to-face community meetings (Asemblea) to prevent conflicts over water stealing or unfair distribution.Frequency: They hold General Assembly meetings at least once or twice a year (usually right before a new cropping season starts). However, the Board of Directors and Terminal Group Leaders meet monthly or as needed.What They Talk About:The Water Schedule: Finalizing dates for when the main irrigation gates will open and close.Canal Maintenance (Brigada): Assigning schedules for farmers to clear mud, weeds, and trash out of the canals.Government Subsidies: Announcing who qualifies for incoming Department of Agriculture (DA) seeds, fertilizers, or financial aid.Financial Reports: Reviewing membership dues, irrigation fees collected, and expenses for canal repairs.Disputes: Resolving issues where a farmer upstream blocked water from reaching a farmer downstream.The System Insight: An Announcements & Meeting Logger module is highly beneficial. It allows leaders to broadcast the cropping calendar via SMS/notifications and log attendance or minutes regarding canal maintenance assignments.Summary Architecture for Your SystemIf you are mapping out an Entity-Relationship Diagram (ERD) or system architecture, your core pillars should look like this:System PillarWhat It TracksWhy It MattersMember RegistryFarmer profile, Membership status, Contact info, Role (Officer vs. Regular Member)Controls access to subsidized seeds and inputs.Land Asset MappingParcel ID, Size (hectares), Tenure (Owned/Rented), Geographic Sector / Turn-Out GroupCrucial for calculating water fees and verifying seed allocations.Cropping & Water SchedulerWater release dates, Expected harvest dates per sectorEnsures farmers log their planting activities in sync with water availability.Maintenance & LogsBrigada tasks, Canal repair logs, Meeting minutes/attendanceTracks community contributions and association transparency.
